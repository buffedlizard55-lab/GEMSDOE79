1. The metric rewards sparse dots, not probability maps

From the official metric definition, with k(d) = max(1 − d/300 m, 0) on the 100 m grid:

TP  = Σ_truth   max_x p(x)·k(d)          (each truth pixel takes its best nearby prediction)
FP  = Σ_x       p(x)·(1 − max_truth k(d)) (every predicted unit pays unless it is on/near truth)
FN  = N_truth − TP
DTI = TP / (TP + 0.2·FP + 0.8·FN)

Known catalogue pixels are masked from scoring (staff clarification), and terms are pooled over the public chunks before the ratio (aggregation clarification).

Two asymmetries follow directly:

  1. TP is a max, FP is a sum. A single dot of value 1 sitting on a straight truth trace credits the pixels at 0, ±100 m and ±200 m with 1, 0.667 and 0.333. Repeating dots every 3 pixels along the trace gives each truth pixel its best kernel value, so each dot earns about 1 + 2×0.667 = 2.33 truth-pixel units of TP while paying at most 1 unit of FP. A continuous map pays FP on every positive pixel and earns no extra TP from neighbours that are already covered. Dotting is the reason almost every high GEMSDOE entry, H33 included, is a binary sparse field.
  2. Break-even probability. Adding a dot with expected TP gain A and FP cost B ≈ 1 improves the score s when A > 0.2·s·(A + B), that is A > 0.2s / (1 − 0.2s). With A ≈ 2.33·P(dot lies on a truth trace): at s = 0.28 a dot is worth placing if P > ≈2.5 %; at s = 0.44 it must be P > ≈4.1 %. (ESTIMATE; it ignores partial-kernel hits and overlap between dots.)

A rigorous lower bound also follows. Write c = TP/N (the share of truth credit captured) and r = FP/N. Then c = s(0.2r + 0.8)/(1 − 0.2s) ≥ 0.8s/(1 − 0.2s), so a public score of 0.2778 needs c ≥ 23.6 % and 0.4413 needs c ≥ 38.4 %.

2. What H33 actually is, and why it won inside the GEMSDOE family

RASTER-MEASUREMENT (evidence/h33_forensics.json, knowledge/why_h33.md): H33 is exactly the D2.8 dot set (44,090 dots) minus the 6,436 dots within 200 m (2 px) of a mapped fault. No dot was added or moved.

USER-REPORTED: D2.8 0.2600 and H33 0.2778.

Under the unverified assumption that the 6,436 removed dots had zero marginal TP, only FP changed. Adjacency to masked catalogue pixels does not establish this: nearby unmapped strands can still earn TP, and the 200 m collar itself is not masked. Then the ESTIMATE is TP·(1/0.2600 − 1/0.2778) = 0.2·6,436. That gives TP ≈ 5.2 k truth-pixel units in the public set and N_truth ≈ 12.8–13.3 k. H33 therefore captures c ≈ 39–41 % of public truth credit with ≈35–37 k FP units.

H33 led the family for three reasons, ranked by our confidence:

  1. It deleted near-catalogue dots, plausibly reducing redundant FP. This is the measured raster change, not proof that every deleted dot was a false positive. Exact mapped pixels are masked; the surrounding 200 m collar is not. Nearby unmapped splays could lose TP when those dots are removed. The reported score pair is consistent with a net gain, but artifact-linked organizer receipts and hidden labels are absent, so the causal explanation remains conditional.
  2. Its dots are already sparse and roughly at budget. The measured budget is about 37.7 k dots. Whether its marginal dot is near break-even is unknown without hidden truth. Additional dots can either help or hurt according to their incremental TP and FP, not their count alone.
  3. Its ranking came from distance-to-fault structure plus scarps, inherited from D2.x. RASTER-MEASUREMENT (evidence/nhr/distance_to_catalogue.json): - The median H33 dot lies 19.6 px (≈1.96 km) from the catalogue. - 69.8 % of its dots are more than 1 km away. - Only 15.0 % are within 500 m.

It is a broad, moderately catalogue-aware field. It is not a tight halo.

None of this proves why the private labels favour H33; the leaderboard pairs are user-reported and the public leaderboard names teams, not files.

4. Can we beat H33? What H79-NHR measured

H79-NHR trains a gradient-boosted regressor on nested hide-and-recover data. Inside each training fold it hides 20 % of catalogue components, buffers them by 3 px and fits the official 300 m kernel credit of the hidden components. It uses 19 supplied bands, multiscale terrain/gravity/magnetic derivatives, 10 lidar scarp bands, 8 radiometric bands and 6 geometry inputs computed only from the visible catalogue. The target therefore is the quantity the dot allocator needs: expected kernel credit for a fault that is missing from the map.

3. Why H33 scored highest (PhD-level mechanism)

Evidence: evidence/h33_forensics.json, evidence/family_lineage_forensics.json, knowledge/why_h33.md (all RASTER-MEASUREMENT unless stated).

Lineage. Every reported family score above 0.2478 descends from one root surface, h19-5. H33 is not a new model: it is the D2.8 dot field with its 6,436 dots within 200 m of the catalogue deleted, leaving 37,654 dots. Along the lineage, the count of dots within 200 m of the catalogue falls 6436 → 3894 → 2545 → 2353 → 0 while the reported score rises 0.2600 → 0.2632 → 0.2708 → 0.2710 → 0.2778 (UNVERIFIED HISTORICAL REPORT for the scores; RASTER-MEASUREMENT for the dot counts).

Why deleting near-catalogue dots helps, in metric terms. DTI's FP term charges each positive pixel p(x)·(1 − max_g k(d)) against the expert truth set, and the catalogue itself is masked pixel-exactly. A dot 100 m from a catalogue trace earns credit only if an expert (unmapped) fault pixel lies within 300 m; dots hugging mapped traces mostly pay FP mass while adding little TPw, because the max-cover in TPw saturates — one dot near a truth pixel already captures most of its credit, and β = 0.8 makes uncovered truth (FNw) four times more expensive per unit than FPw. H33's edit therefore does two things at once: it removes nearly pure-FP mass, and the fixed budget is re-expressed as a dot field whose median distance to the catalogue is 1.96 km, with 69.8 % of dots beyond 1 km (RASTER-MEASUREMENT, evidence/ber/distance_diagnostics.json reproduces this profile: median 19.6 px). In other words, H33 wins not by modelling faults better but by spending its fixed budget where unmapped-fault discovery is geometrically possible and refusing to spend it re-tracing the catalogue.

Why the H19-5 root is hard to beat. The genuinely independent family lanes (GEMSDOE70 at a reported 0.2478, forward 3 px overlap vs H33 only 0.548; GEMSDOE49 at 0.2376, overlap 0.434 — overlaps RASTER-MEASUREMENT, scores UNVERIFIED) prove expert truth has large mass off the H19-5 lines. Yet on the offline hide-and-recover instrument, H19-5-lineage dots recover withheld catalogue components better than any single-sensor evidence ranking tried in this repo (H33 fold-fair control 0.0961 / 0.0989 HOLDOUT-DTI, reproduced exactly in three independent lanes). The likely reason is corroboration: H19-5 was built from multiple independent lineament extractions, so its lines encode cross-sensor agreement that a per-pixel tabular model over the same rasters does not reconstruct — agreement is precisely what separates fault scarps from the named mimics (paleo-shorelines, canyon margins, lithologic contacts). G79-BER's stratified results (§5) are direct evidence: within 1 km of the catalogue our per-band models match the control, but beyond 2 km — where single- sensor evidence thins and corroboration matters most — they lose in every band on both seeds.

The trap this sets. The pooled holdout rewards catalogue proximity (withheld catalogue components sit near mapped systems; 98 % of withheld truth pixels this session lie within 5 km, 83 % within 2 km). H79-NHR exploited that with distance/density geometry, beat the control (0.1431 / 0.1522 HOLDOUT-DTI), and duplicated 26 registry fields whose reported public scores are 0.016–0.143 — i.e. the offline instrument's optimum is a known poor leaderboard region. Any lane that simply maximises pooled HOLDOUT-DTI will re-enter that trap. G79-BER was designed against it explicitly; that it still lost to the control is the scientific content of this session.

6. Uniqueness and amendment D-2 (REGISTRY-MEASUREMENT)

The literal duplicate rule (" > 70 % of dots within 3 px of any registry raster's positive support") is unsatisfiable in this registry (IR-79-53: space-filling priors flag even uniform-random dots). With the user's standing instruction that uniqueness must be decidable, this session adopted amendment D-2 prospectively, before any BER result (reports/protocol_amendment_D2_20261022.md), implemented once in the shared tool (vendor/gems57/uniqueness.py, compare_array_to_registry_d2, pin updated in registry/template_pin.json, 7 unit tests): a candidate is a duplicate iff it is byte/decoded-identical, or Spearman ρ > 0.90, or its 3 px forward overlap against a prior's dot-equivalent set exceeds 0.70 and the chance-corrected overlap (ov − h)/(1 − h) (h = the prior halo's share of allowed cells) also exceeds 0.70; if the halo saturates (h ≥ 0.999999) exact-cell overlap > 0.70 decides instead. D-2 does not resurrect stopped lanes (NHR's worst corrected overlap is 0.777 — still a duplicate). Wording is always "unique under D-2", never literal-rule unique; literal overlaps are reported alongside (78 literal firings for BER, as for any map in this registry).

Surface scan (pre-placement, as protocol requires): 986 of 992 registry rasters comparable. The 6 exceptions are named in the evidence: four CRS/transform mismatches (including two SCHEMA-DEMO files), one corrupt TIFF (smoke.tif, missing StripOffsets), one grid/band mismatch (GEMS36_FORMAT_TEST_NOT_SUBMISSION). The precise metric argument

Official formula:

  • k(d) = max(1 - d / 300 m, 0).
  • TP = sum_truth max_prediction[p(x) k(d)].
  • FP = sum_prediction p(x) (1 - max_truth k(d)).
  • FN = number_of_truth_pixels - TP.
  • DTI = TP / (TP + 0.2 FP + 0.8 FN).

The staff clarification excludes exact known-fault pixels, not a 200 m buffer around them. The aggregation clarification requires pooling terms first rather than averaging fold DTI.

A dot can cover several truth pixels. Therefore its marginal true-positive credit A is not generally bounded by its own nearest-truth credit q. For a new unit dot, its false-positive increment is B = 1 - q. If the current score is s, adding it improves DTI precisely when

A > 0.2 * s * (A + B).

Only in the special case A = q does this simplify to q > 0.2*s. We do not use that special-case threshold as a universal claim. Removing a redundant dot with negligible lost coverage but positive false-positive mass can improve DTI; this is why thinning is plausible. But removing a useful dot can hurt recall. Geometry alone cannot determine the hidden-truth trade-off.

The -zeros encoding is a format policy, not a scientific discovery. Replacing outside-footprint NaN with finite zero does not add in-footprint geological evidence. Without the rejected bytes and server receipt, neither NaN nor a sentinel can be proven to have caused the user's earlier range error. Our writer simply rejects nonfinite/out-of-range outputs, never clips them, and checks the serialized result and single-TIFF ZIP.

Why a larger score cannot be inferred from this family table

  1. Confounding: many submissions inherit the same ranking or dot set. They are not independent scientific replications.
  2. Distribution shift: withheld existing faults are not the organizer's new expert-labelled faults. High catalogue recovery may select familiar exposed faults and miss weak, covered structures.
  3. Multiplicity: dozens of ideas and emission settings compared on one proxy can overfit that proxy even when each model is superficially cross-validated.
  4. Measurement limits: a 100 m grid does not imply independent 100 m airborne observations. USGS reports Area 2 flight lines spaced 400 m apart. Fine-scale registration lags can reflect interpolation, levelling or flight geometry.
  5. Novelty gate degeneracy: under the supplied literal support convention, one whole-footprint prior rejects every nonempty candidate. A filename or byte hash cannot resolve this.

Additional primary-literature review — implications, not new experiments

Reviewed on 2026-10-21, after the mandatory E1 stop. This review did not change features, consume another experiment, label new faults or authorize a second lane. It provides context for interpreting failures and planning an explicitly authorized future study.

Hermant, Kiersnowski & Bellanger (Stanford Geothermal Workshop, 2025)

Using Deep Learning to Map Quaternary Faults in Western USA — full paper. All eight parsed chunks, including methods, discussion and references, were read. This is a primary author publication hosted by Stanford, not an organizer receipt, USGS endorsement, or evidence that its model was reproduced here.

What is useful for this problem:

  • The paper distinguishes incomplete, regionally heterogeneous public mapping from true fault absence. Its Figure 2 illustrates mapping discrepancies; this supports treating catalogue zeros as unlabelled, not certified negatives.
  • The authors use approximately 10 m elevation/slope and Sentinel-2 B8A imagery, not this lane's 100 m magnetic/gravity stack. They restrict their elevation selection to lidar-derived areas after observing slope artifacts elsewhere (§3.1). Resampling a coarse grid cannot manufacture that independent evidence.
  • Their §5.1 procedure randomly splits fault-containing image tiles before data augmentation. Their paper metrics are PR-AUC and weighted focal IoU, not the competition's pooled distance-weighted Tversky. Neither these metrics nor qualitative figures establish our blocked hide-and-recover performance. A random tile split is not a substitute for our required buffered whole-segment split; this is a protocol distinction, not proof that their results leaked.
  • The authors report overfitting and explicitly identify paleo-shorelines, canyon margins, streams and other scarps as fault mimics (§6–7, Figures 8–9). A long, clear lineament is therefore not sufficient geological evidence. Automatically excluding every shoreline or drainage feature is unsafe too, because faults can be co-located with them.
  • Faults can conduct or impede fluid flow (§2). Better fault detection is not proof of a permeable, hot or commercially productive reservoir. Their expert manual correction is not an automated submission-validation method; it was not substituted for our automatic gates.

Practical consequence: a future, separately authorized morphological lane would need independent data provenance/resolution checks, a non-fault-mimic control, and buffered spatial tests. Proprietary FaultSEG weights and its tracing algorithm are not claimed to be available. This project neither implemented nor scored a CNN from that publication.

  • Great Basin Center for Geothermal Energy — INGENIOUS project describes integrated structural, geophysical, thermal and exploration work; its primary project/publication listing was reviewed. It does not establish independent hidden-test labels or commercial discovery from a raster alone.

The release lists a public 6.5 MB grid ZIP and metadata/readme.
